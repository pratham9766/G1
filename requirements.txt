G1
Emergency Medical History Intelligence Platform
Master PRD + TRD + App Flow + UI/UX Brief + Backend Schema + Implementation Plan

Core principle: G1 does not diagnose, prescribe, or recommend treatment. It reconstructs and summarizes existing, authorized medical information into a fast, evidence-linked brief for clinical use.

Version 1.0 • September 2026

1. Executive Summary
G1 is a privacy-first healthcare web application intended to help authorized clinicians understand a patient's critical medical history quickly, especially in time-sensitive or emergency settings. The system accepts authorized medical records such as discharge summaries, prescriptions, diagnostic reports, laboratory reports, and hospital records; reconstructs a longitudinal clinical history; detects conflicts and uncertainty; and presents an evidence-linked brief.
The product is built around three primary users: Patient, Doctor, and Hospital. Patients control access and consent, doctors consume the evidence-linked brief, and hospitals govern verified users, policies, integrations, and audits. G1 is planned as an ABDM-compatible HIU-side intelligence layer rather than a replacement for ABHA/ABDM or a central health-record repository.
ABDM alignment from the uploaded guidelines: M1 covers ABHA creation/capture/verification, M2 covers HIP services, and M3 covers HIU services for viewing a patient's medical history with complete consent. For G1, M3 is the strategically important integration milestone; M1 may be used where needed, while M2 is not required for the first version unless G1 later becomes a record-issuing/sharing system.

2. Problem Statement
Emergency clinicians often have limited time to review long, fragmented, repetitive, and inconsistent medical histories.
Relevant information may be distributed across hospitals, labs, prescriptions, PDFs, scanned images, and structured digital records.
Critical facts such as allergies, current medications, major procedures, implants, or recent abnormalities can be buried in unrelated information.
A generic AI summary is unsafe if it cannot prove where each clinical statement came from or if it silently resolves conflicting records.
ABDM enables consent-driven health information exchange, but clinicians still need a fast interpretation layer to understand the retrieved records.

9.2 Client-Server and Backend Service Architecture
G1 primarily follows a client-server architecture. The responsive Patient, Doctor, and Hospital web clients communicate with the server over secure HTTPS APIs. The server contains application logic, consent enforcement, ABDM/HMIS integration, clinical processing, AI services, audit functions, and secure data access. Within the server, modules are separated by clear service boundaries so that the system can begin as a modular monolith and later extract high-load components into independent services if required.
Architecture classification: Overall = Client-Server; application structure = Three-Tier; backend organization = Modular Service-Oriented; background processing = Event-Driven/Asynchronous; healthcare interoperability = FHIR/ABDM; security = Zero-Trust and privacy-segregated data architecture.

10.3 Data Integrity Rules
Identity data and clinical data are joined only by an internal randomized patient UUID/reference.
Clinical facts cannot be promoted to CONFIRMED unless at least one valid evidence pointer exists.
Conflicting facts remain independent and are grouped by a Conflict record; the system does not overwrite one with the other.
Consent/access validity is checked server-side before every protected clinical read.
Original source hashes are stored to detect tampering and enable provenance verification.
Production must use dynamic source connectors and event/callback-based refresh where available; static JSON is limited to fixtures, demos, and unit tests.

11.1 AI Safety Rules
The LLM cannot create new clinical facts; it may only verbalize structured facts that already have evidence.
No unsupported claim is displayed as a fact.
No diagnosis, prescription, treatment recommendation, or doctor ranking is generated.
Model/extractor/prompt versions are logged for each generated brief.
Model updates require benchmark evaluation and controlled rollout.
Identifiable health records are not sent to general-purpose external AI services by default.
Training on patient clinical records is disabled unless a future, separately governed, legally valid program explicitly permits it.

14.1 High-Assurance Privacy & Security Architecture
For G1, standard healthcare security is not sufficient. The production architecture should follow a high-assurance design in which compromise of a single account, service, database, or AI component is not enough to expose both a patient identity and the complete clinical history. Security controls must be enforced at the identity, network, application, data, integration, AI, and operational layers.
High-Assurance Control
Required Design
Zero-trust service access
Mutual service identity, short-lived credentials, authorization on every call, no implicit trust based on network location.
Identity / clinical segregation
Direct identifiers live in an Identity Vault; clinical facts live in a Clinical Vault; linkage uses an internal randomized Patient_UUID.
Field-level protection
Tokenize or encrypt high-value identifiers such as ABHA references, mobile, email, DOB and other direct identifiers where operationally feasible.
Key isolation
Use KMS/HSM-managed keys with rotation, strict access policies, and tenant/patient-level key separation where feasible.
Just-in-time access
Prefer time-bound clinician access derived from current role, affiliation, purpose, consent scope and expiry rather than permanent broad access.
Privileged access management
Platform and infrastructure administrators use separate privileged accounts, MFA, approval workflows and session logging.
Tamper-evident audit
Use append-only/WORM-capable storage or cryptographic hash chaining for sensitive audit records.
No PHI in telemetry
Never place health data in URLs, analytics events, crash reporting, generic logs, traces or third-party monitoring payloads.
DLP and bulk-read detection
Detect abnormal exports, unusual query volume, bulk patient access and suspicious cross-tenant activity.
Secure sessions
Short-lived access tokens, refresh-token rotation, device/session revocation, secure cookies and replay protections.
Signed integrations
ABDM/HMIS callbacks and webhooks must be authenticated, signed where supported, correlation-checked and protected against replay.
Hardened file ingestion
MIME/content validation, malware scanning, size limits, decompression-bomb protection, parser isolation and defenses against malicious PDF/image payloads.
AI context isolation
Patient contexts, vector stores, caches and prompts must be isolated so one patient cannot influence or expose another patient context.
Document prompt-injection defense
Instructions embedded in uploaded medical documents are always treated as untrusted document content, never as system instructions.
AI output enforcement
Structured schema validation plus claim-to-evidence validation occurs before model output is visible to clinicians.
Automated retention
Retention and deletion workflows are tied to consent, legal obligations and explicit policy rather than manual cleanup.
Secure SDLC
SAST, DAST, dependency and container scanning, secret scanning, SBOM generation, patch SLAs and recurring VAPT.
Break-glass governance
Any emergency override, if later legally and operationally permitted, requires explicit justification, elevated logging, alerts and retrospective review. It must not be assumed in V1.
14.2 Privacy-Preserving AI Processing Boundary
The AI layer must never receive unrestricted access to the identity database. Identifiable source records are processed inside a trusted boundary; direct identifiers are minimized or pseudonymized before AI processing where clinically possible. The model receives only the authorized clinical context needed for the requested brief, and generated statements are checked against evidence before display.
Identity Vault → Patient_UUID → Clinical Vault → Pseudonymized/Minimum-Necessary AI Context → Private Model → Schema Validator → Claim-to-Evidence Validator → Authorized Doctor UI
High-assurance rule: No single compromised user, service, database, or AI component should be sufficient to expose both a patient's identity and complete clinical history.

20. Source Basis and Compliance Notes
This document is grounded in the two ABDM/NDHM documents supplied for the project: (1) Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission, Guidelines for Health Information Providers, Health Repository Providers, Health Information Users and Personal Health Records Apps, version dated 24 October 2022; and (2) NDHM Sandbox Enabling Framework V1.0, dated 18 August 2020.
The 2022 HIP/HIU guideline states that ABDM provides interoperability building blocks; identifies HIP, HIU, HIE-CM, HPR and HFR roles; describes patient-controlled consent; states that health records remain with the respective HIP rather than being centrally stored by NHA; and defines M1/M2/M3 integration milestones.
The 2020 Sandbox framework is older and should be treated as historical guidance for security, testing, sandbox process, and certification concepts. Current ABDM Sandbox v3 documentation and current NHA/MeitY/legal requirements must be checked before implementation or production onboarding.
Final product rule: if G1 cannot prove where a clinical statement came from, it must not present that statement as a confirmed fact.
